In a recent study published in Food & Function, researchers evaluated whether 8 weeks of walnut consumption affects mood states in young adults and whether these effects are associated with urinary 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA), cortisol, and urolithin levels.

Why walnuts may influence mood

Mental health disorders affect 13.9% of the global population, with the burden expected to rise among young people.  Due to the high energy and nutrient requirements of the brain, diet has emerged as a lifestyle factor pertinent to mental health.

Serotonin is a neurotransmitter involved in mood regulation and is produced from the dietary amino acid tryptophan.  Its main metabolite, 5-HIAA, can be measured in urine as an indicator of serotonin metabolism.  Omega-3 fatty acids, tryptophan, fiber, and polyphenols—including ellagitannins, which the microbiota in the gut transform into urolithins—are all abundant in walnuts. These metabolites may indicate biological responses to walnut consumption; however, more research is required to clarify the connection between walnut consumption and mood and metabolic pathways. evaluating the effects of daily walnut consumption Adults between the ages of 20 and 35 participated in the open-label, randomized crossover trial for 18 weeks. Researchers recruited participants from December 2021 to February 2022.
 Candidates were required to maintain moderate physical activity and have a BMI between 19 and 26 kg/m2. Exclusion criteria included nut allergies, acute or chronic illnesses, smoking, use of drugs or dietary supplements, restrictive diets, shift work, and inability to provide written informed consent.  36 of the 84 people who had been screened participated; five of them dropped out, leaving 31 people who completed both arms. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov after receiving approval from the Ethics Committee of the University of Barcelona. For the next eight weeks, participants were randomly assigned to either consume 40 grams of walnuts with dinner or not eat nuts or products containing nuts. After a 2-week washout period, participants crossed over to the alternate condition.
 A nutritionist provided standardized guidance for participants to follow a Mediterranean-style diet in both conditions.  The Profile of Mood States Questionnaire (POMS) was used to measure mood, while validated Spanish questionnaires were used to measure perceived stress, well-being, and trait food cravings. First-morning urine samples were obtained for 5-HIAA and cortisol analysis using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), normalized to creatinine, and for urolithins using high-performance liquid chromatography coupled to quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (HPLC-QTOF-MS).  Repeated-measures correlations and linear mixed-effects models were utilized throughout. Consuming walnuts reduced overall mood disturbance. The average age of the 31 participants was 24 years, and 90.3% of them were women. Body fat percentage was 25.7% and mean BMI was 21.6 kg/m2. Total mood disturbance was 4.85 points lower in the walnut condition than in the control condition. Anger and fatigue also decreased, but these differences did not remain statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons.  Regarding friendliness, vigor, perceived stress, and well-being, there were no significant differences. The anticipation of relief from negative states and feelings after eating and the guilt associated with cravings or giving in to them showed initial reductions in food-craving traits. However, after multiple-testing correction, neither result remained statistically significant. In repeated-measures analyses, higher scores in both domains were associated with increases in anger, fatigue, or total mood disturbance.
 While there was no significant difference between the two conditions, walnut consumption resulted in a significant rise in urinary 5-HIAA. There was a correlation between lower total mood disturbance and higher urinary 5-HIAA. The association with less fatigue is also mentioned in the paper, though the significance is internal inconsistent. The assessed food-hungry domains did not correlate with higher urinary 5-HIAA levels. During walnut consumption, both urolithin A and urolithin B concentrations in the urine were also significantly higher. Additionally, there was a correlation between these two metabolites and 5-HIAA concentrations in the urine at the same time. BMI, body fat percentage, and physical activity did not differ significantly.  Diet quality was modestly but significantly higher during the walnut condition than during the control condition.
 Urinary 5-HIAA reflects whole-body serotonin metabolism and can be influenced by peripheral serotonin production, diet, intestinal metabolism, and renal excretion.  Because neither central serotonergic function nor brain serotonin were measured in this study, the observed associations cannot establish a link between walnuts and changes in brain serotonin activity. Interpretation is also limited by the small sample, women's predominance, and open-label design, which may have introduced expectancy bias. Since no additional assessment was carried out prior to the second intervention period, the researchers were unable to directly confirm whether or not participants had reverted to comparable physiological and behavioral states following the washout. During the control condition, which may have been different from their usual diets, participants also avoided all nuts. The young study population also limits how readily the findings apply to other groups.
 What the findings mean for the metabolism of serotonin and mood The randomized crossover trial found that consuming 40 g of walnuts daily for 8 weeks was associated with lower total mood disturbance and higher urinary 5-HIAA in healthy young adults.  Urinary urolithin A and B also increased and were correlated with concurrent changes in urinary 5-HIAA.
 There were no significant differences in well-being, positive mood, perceived stress levels, or urinary cortisol levels, according to the study. These results, taken as a whole, point to a possible connection between whole-body serotonin metabolism and microbial metabolites derived from walnuts; however, the underlying pathways remain a mystery. Further research with larger, more diverse populations and longer intervention periods is needed.
